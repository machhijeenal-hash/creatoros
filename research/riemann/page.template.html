I tried to solve the Riemann Hypothesis.

I did not. Nobody has, in 167 years — and no amount of compute changes that, because the hypothesis quantifies over infinitely many zeros while every computation inspects only finitely many. So instead of pretending, here is the part that is real: four experiments that can be run on a laptop, the numbers they produced, and the precise line where each of the five standard proof strategies collapses.

The vertical dashed line is σ = ½. RH says: every hole in this strip sits exactly on it. The functional equation ξ(s) = ξ(1−s) already forces the picture to be symmetric about that line, which is why the holes come in mirror pairs — the question is whether the pair ever separates.

02

The oscillation on the line

Z(t) = eiθ(t)ζ(½+it) is real for real t, so the zeros on the critical line are just the zeros of a real function of one variable.

Riemann–Siegel Z(t) — every sign change is a zero of ζ on σ = ½

zeros shown: 0

The vertical strokes are the zeros γn located by mpmath at 25 digits and re-found here by bisection on sign changes. Look at the spacing — irregular, but with a very specific rigidity: gaps repel zeros from each other. That repulsion is the fingerprint of the zeros behaving like eigenvalues of a random Hermitian operator, the strongest hint anyone has for why RH should be true.

03

How do you know you didn't miss one?

You count. Twice. Once over the whole strip, once only on the line. If the two numbers agree, there is no room left for a zero to hide.

The winding number comes out at e.g. 52.0062 instead of 52: the excess is pure discretisation of the contour (the boundary is sampled every 0.02). Rounded to an integer, it is exact — and rounding is legitimate here because the winding number of a closed contour is an integer.

why this is the real method

The rectangle [−1.5, 2] × [0.5, T] contains no poles of ζ (the only pole, s = 1, has imaginary part 0) and no trivial zeros (they sit at −2, −4, …). So the winding number of ζ around its boundary counts every nontrivial zero with 0 < Im s < T.

Meanwhile the sign changes of Z(t) count only the zeros on the line. Equality of the two counts leaves no zero unaccounted for.

This is exactly how the published verifications reach 1013 zeros (van de Lune–te Riele–Winter 1986, Gourdon 2004) — with interval arithmetic replacing floating point, and Turing's method replacing the contour walk. My version is the toy-sized, floating-point cousin: it demonstrates the mechanism, it does not extend the frontier by a single zero.

04

Why anyone cares

The primes are a sum of the zeros. Literally.

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − log 2π − ½log(1−x−2)

rms error — · max —

The staircase is the exact Chebyshev function ψ(x) = Σpk ≤ x log p, computed from a sieve — no zeta involved. The smooth curve is built only from zeros. Each zero ρ = ½ + iγ contributes a wave x1/2cos(γ log x)/|ρ|, and stacking them rebuilds the primes. That is the whole argument for RH: if some zero had Re ρ = ½ + δ, its wave would grow like x½+δ and the primes would visibly drift off the line x. Every zero found so far is exactly on the knife edge δ = 0 — which is what "the primes are as well-behaved as a random process permits" means, made numerical.

This is a local check: it confirms that at every known zero ordinate the function really is zero on the line and strictly nonzero a little to either side. It says nothing about ordinates nobody has looked at, and there are infinitely many of those — which is precisely the gap between "verified" and "proved".

06

Why none of this is a proof

Five strategies, five reasons they fail — and one of them is not a reason a computer can fix.

A proof must cover all zeros. A computation covers the first N. For any N you choose, there are infinitely many ordinates beyond it, and RH makes a claim about every one of them. That is not a technicality — it is the reason the problem survived a century and a half of Moore's law.

route 01 — brute force

Verify more zeros

From the first handful of zeros (Gram, 1903) to 1013 (Gourdon, 2004). Every single one sits on the line.

Fails because 1013 is 0% of infinity. Any finite verification, however large, is compatible with a counterexample at 1014.

route 02 — positivity

Li's criterion / Weil positivity

RH ⇔ λn ≥ 0 for all n, where λn = Σρ [1 − (1 − 1/ρ)n]; equivalently, a certain quadratic form is positive semidefinite.

Fails because positivity must hold on an infinite-dimensional space. Numerics check a finite truncation, and every truncation that has ever been tested is positive — including for hypotheses that are false. This is where de Branges' attempts died too.

route 03 — elementary reformulation

Mertens: |M(x)| ≤ √x

M(x) = Σn≤x μ(n). Mertens conjectured |M(x)| ≤ √x, which implies RH.

Fails — and it is known to fail (Odlyzko–te Riele, 1985): M(x)/√x exceeds 1 somewhere below 101020. RH needs the weaker M(x) = O(x½+ε), and no elementary argument gets there. Note the lesson: a plausible pattern, confirmed numerically for millions of x, was false.

route 04 — density

Zero-density estimates

Bound the number N(σ,T) of zeros with Re ρ ≥ σ. Ingham (1940) stood for 84 years; Guth–Maynard (2024) broke it with decoupling: N(σ,T) ≤ T(30/13)(1−σ)+o(1).

Fails structurally: any bound of the shape Tc(1−σ) allows zeros off the line. You can push the proven proportion of on-line zeros up (Levinson ⅓ in 1974, Conrey 40% in 1989, ≈41.6% since, 67.25% reported in 2026) but never to 100%. Maynard himself: there is "no pathway" from these techniques to the hypothesis.

route 05 — spectral

Hilbert–Pólya: find the operator

If the zeros are the eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator, they are automatically real — i.e. confined to σ = ½. The statistics (GUE) scream that such an operator exists.

Fails because nobody has produced the operator, and no amount of zero statistics determines one. The gap-repulsion we can measure is evidence for the analogy, not a construction. (The same gap: "random matrix theory predicts it" is not "it is proved".)

what would count

A proof, if it arrives

Either (a) a positivity theorem for a quadratic form / operator with infinitely many degrees of freedom, or (b) an exact evaluation of a sum over all zeros, or (c) something nobody has thought of.

What would not count: more zeros, a longer computation, a neural network that fits the zeros, or a pattern that holds for the first million cases. None of these can address the infinite tail — and a page like this one is, by construction, in that list.

the record, in order

    the honest summary

    I ran the experiments that exist. They agree with RH everywhere they can look. That is worth exactly what it sounds like: strong evidence about a finite region, and no information about the infinite remainder.

    If you want a proof from this page, the only useful thing here is the negative map: the five routes above are the ones that have been tried hardest, and the failure modes are structural, not computational.

    The one datum I find genuinely hopeful is not a zero count — it is the pair correlation. The zeros behave like eigenvalues of something. Finding that something is the open problem, and it is not a problem that a bigger computer closes.

    precision, honestly

    The Python study runs at 25 decimal digits (mpmath). The browser cannot: it re-implements ζ by Euler–Maclaurin summation and agrees with mpmath to about 4 × 10−13 on the tested grid — plenty to draw a picture, nowhere near enough to certify a zero-free region.

    A certified computation would need interval arithmetic and Turing's method, which is a different order of engineering. The point of this page is the shape of the argument, not a new decimal digit.
